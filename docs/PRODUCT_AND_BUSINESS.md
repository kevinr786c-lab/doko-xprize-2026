# Product and Business

## The operating thesis

Doko is one adaptable operating ecosystem for small medical practices. It combines operational software, digital presence, implementation support, bounded AI, and progressively developed medical-supply sourcing and fulfillment.

The product is built around two principles:

> Build only where Doko adds meaningful value; integrate what is already solved well.

> Observe first. Build small. Validate with real users. Expand only when the workflow proves useful.

This matters because a gynecology practice, a cardiology practice, and a dermatology practice do not need exactly the same tools. Doko keeps a reliable shared core while allowing specialty and clinic-specific capabilities to be added after observation and validation.

## Founder context

My experience did not begin in technology. It came from hands-on hospital supply operations, warehouse and inventory control, supplier interaction, replenishment, invoice handling, and medical-supply sourcing. I also worked with supplies, instruments, and equipment; supported cycle counts and logistics; handled receiving, shipping, and invoices; and helped prepare surgical kits.

That experience taught me to think in operational dependencies: what must be available, who is responsible, what happens when a step fails, and how a process affects the next person. I later applied that way of thinking to software through AI-assisted learning, documentation, experimentation, and direct observation inside clinics.

I am not a traditionally trained software engineer. AI tools helped me learn and implement, but they did not decide the product. Scope and operating boundaries came from real workflows, deterministic controls, testing, and human judgment.

## The problem

Small clinics often operate through disconnected tools and undocumented routines. The doctor may use a calendar, the assistant may coordinate patients through calls and messages, and important knowledge may exist only in one person's habits. Fixed platforms can force a clinic into a generic workflow, while custom systems can become expensive or difficult to maintain.

Doko addresses the space between those extremes:

- retain mature infrastructure where it already works;
- add a practical operating layer around it;
- document clinic-specific processes;
- train and support the people who use them;
- turn only repeated, validated needs into optional software capabilities.

## Current offering

### Production software

- daily appointment operations for doctors and assistants;
- Google Calendar as the integrated scheduling foundation;
- bounded Gmail confirmation workflows;
- patient portal and public medical directory;
- clinic-scoped, bounded Doko assistants;
- Mi Centro for setup, implementation, protocols, and operator controls.

### Operational implementation

Doko is not delivered only as login credentials. Implementation can include observing the clinic, documenting its current process, comparing it with a reference protocol, identifying missing access or tools, training the assistant, and following up after adoption.

The reference protocol is not a universal medical rule and is not used to rank employees. It is a starting point that the doctor can adopt, adapt, reject, or leave undefined. A clinic-specific version becomes authoritative only after review and approval.

### Digital presence

Doko can publish approved information through `doko.lat`, clinic pages, location links, services, and booking access. This improves discoverability and patient orientation, but it does not guarantee patient acquisition.

## How a new specialty is incorporated

1. I observe the clinic and interview the doctor and assistant.
2. I identify the shared Doko core and the specialty-specific friction.
3. The workflow is documented as a draft protocol or controlled experiment.
4. The clinic reviews and adapts the process.
5. The workflow is tested in real use.
6. Only a repeated, safe, maintainable need becomes an optional system capability.

The first production specialty is gynecology. The future goal is to work with one or more founding physicians in additional specialties, not to claim those specialties are already supported.

Founding physicians are real users and early operational validation collaborators, not legal cofounders.

## Validation and traction

Doko has two paying gynecology practices using the system in real operations. A July 27, 2026 snapshot showed 330 appointments visible for one practice and 87 for the other, or 417 across both.

This volume validates that Doko can participate in a busy daily workflow. It does not prove that Doko generated the patients, nor does it establish performance for every medical specialty.

The strongest traction is behavioral:

- an assistant adopted Doko as the preferred operating view;
- clinic feedback directly produced workflow changes;
- doctors requested additional tools after using the core product;
- one doctor recommended Doko to other physicians;
- recurring subscriptions demonstrate willingness to pay.

## Business model

Doko is bootstrapped, with no outside investment. The model has three clearly separated sources:

### 1. Clinic subscriptions — current

Recurring subscriptions support the operating platform, hosting, maintenance, bounded AI usage, and continued improvement.

### 2. Implementation services — current but distinct

Some clinics may request setup, training, operational documentation, or digital-presence services beyond the subscription. These are disclosed separately when they are not subscription revenue.

### 3. Doko Suffy margin — future

Doko Suffy is Doko's integrated medical-supply sourcing and fulfillment capability, designed to grow progressively as the software operation builds recurring revenue, clinic relationships, operational knowledge, and trust.

My hospital and logistics experience is directly relevant here. Many physicians already work with sales representatives, but a representative is normally limited to the brands and catalog of one company. Doko Suffy is intended to search across appropriate suppliers, compare available options, review quality and suitability, and coordinate fulfillment without being dependent on one manufacturer.

Its proposed progression is:

1. controlled base catalog;
2. specialized sourcing and alternatives;
3. trusted supplier network;
4. internal purchasing, lot, warehouse, and delivery operations;
5. organic expansion driven by real clinic demand.

Suffy inventory refers to Doko's future sourcing and logistics operation, not inventory stored in clinic drawers. No future Suffy revenue is counted as current revenue.

## Sustainability and reinvestment

The operating loop is:

```text
subscriptions
  -> stable infrastructure and support
  -> validated product improvements
  -> stronger clinic relationships
  -> additional recurring revenue and observed demand
  -> progressive Suffy sourcing and fulfillment
  -> additional revenue opportunities
  -> reinvestment in the same ecosystem
```

This is the business strategy, not a promise of guaranteed growth. The early advantage is cost discipline: current infrastructure is inexpensive relative to subscription revenue, and new capability is added only when a real workflow justifies its maintenance cost.

## Economic opportunity

If Doko grows, the work should not remain dependent on one person. Potential roles include:

- clinic implementation and training;
- specialty workflow documentation;
- customer support and operational follow-up;
- supplier research and purchasing;
- warehouse, lot, and inventory control for Suffy;
- delivery and local fulfillment;
- software maintenance, security, and quality assurance.

These are future opportunities, not current headcount claims.

## Five-year direction

**FUTURE DIRECTION:** build a trusted operating ecosystem for small practices in Tijuana and then expand carefully to similar markets. Doko should be associated with reliable clinic operations, practical implementation, approved digital presence, and useful supply support.

The target is not growth at any cost. The target is a maintainable product, recurring revenue, documented specialty knowledge, and an operation that can train future staff to deliver the same quality without depending entirely on the founder.

## Defensibility

Doko's defensibility is not a single feature. It is the accumulated combination of:

- direct clinic relationships and observed workflows;
- versioned implementation protocols;
- specialty-specific operational knowledge;
- a deterministic core with bounded AI;
- integration with mature infrastructure instead of unnecessary replacement;
- founder experience in medical supplies and logistics;
- continuous validation with real users.

## Risks and boundaries

- Two clinics are meaningful validation but remain a small customer base.
- Current production experience is concentrated in gynecology.
- The founder still performs most implementation and support.
- Suffy is not yet commercially validated.
- AI must remain bounded and must never replace medical judgment.
- Digital presence can improve discoverability but cannot guarantee new patients.

These limits are disclosed because sustainable growth depends on recognizing them early.
